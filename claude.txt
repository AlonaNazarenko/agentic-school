  is it a time series problem?

⏺ Yes. Option #4 is a time series problem, specifically a multivariate
  spatio-temporal one.

  Why it counts

  - The data is a time series. Each oblast has its own series of alert states
    over time, such as 0/1 every 5 minutes or a list of start and end
    timestamps. With 24–25 oblasts, that's 24–25 parallel series.
  - The forecast depends on the past. You predict the future, P(Kyiv on alert in
    [t, t+30 min]), using only information before t. That's the definition of
    time series forecasting.
  - The "graph" just adds a spatial dimension. It doesn't replace the time
    dimension. It models how one region's series affects another's, similar to a
    VAR model but with a network structure.

  Two ways to frame it

  Approach: Hawkes process
  Time series form: Event times (alert starts) for each oblast, treated as a
  point process in continuous time
  Classic counterpart: A point-process time series model, where each past event
  raises the near-term risk
  ────────────────────────────────────────
  Approach: Graph neural network (e.g. STGCN, GraphWaveNet)
  Time series form: A tensor of shape [time × oblast × features] at a regular
  step (for example 5 or 15 minutes)
  Classic counterpart: A multivariate time series where oblasts are the
  variables
  and their neighbours act as extra inputs

  How to make it clearly time series in your report

  1. Include standard time series baselines: persistence, seasonal naive (same
     hour yesterday or last week), and VAR or logistic regression on lagged
     values from all oblasts.
  2. Show ACF/PACF plots, daily and weekly seasonality, and cross-correlation
     between oblasts at different lags. For example, Kharkiv often goes on alert
     before Kyiv, with the lag typically around X minutes.
  3. Use rolling-origin (walk-forward) evaluation on the latest period.
  4. Then show that the spatial model beats those baselines.

  Simpler alternative with the same idea: if a GNN or Hawkes model feels too
  heavy, use LightGBM. Give it features like "number of neighbouring oblasts on
  alert in the last 15/30/60 minutes" and "minutes since an alert started in a
  neighbour". It's still spatio-temporal forecasting, but much easier to build
  and explain.

❯ okay what is the more complex model which can be used?

⏺ Here are the more complex options, from the most practical to the most
  research-level. The 5-minute alert data is already in place for all of them.

  1. LightGBM with spatio-temporal features ⭐ (recommended as the main model)

  - Inputs at each forecast time t:
    - the oblast's own history: minutes since its last alert, alerts in the last
      1/6/24 hours
    - neighbours: minutes since Kyivska went on alert, number of oblasts on
      alert now
    - the "attack wave": change in that number over the last 15/30 minutes
    - hour and day of week
  - Why this one:
    - It's usually the strongest model on tabular time series.
    - It's fast and easy to keep leakage-free.
    - Feature importance shows which oblast warns which.
  - Expected result: it should clearly beat climatology, because sNaive showed
    the signal isn't in seasonality.

  2. Multivariate Hawkes process (most interpretable)

  - Each alert in oblast A temporarily raises the alert risk in oblast B. The
    model learns how strong that effect is (α_AB) and how quickly it fades (β).
  - The fitted α matrix is a propagation map of how alerts spread between
    oblasts, which works well as a figure in your report.
  - It's a natural fit for event data like alert start times.
  - It's usually a bit weaker than LightGBM at prediction, but much easier to
    explain.

  3. Spatio-temporal graph neural network (STGCN / GConvGRU / Graph WaveNet)

  - Nodes are oblasts and edges are borders plus the lift values from the
    analysis. The input is the alert state tensor (time × oblast × features).
  - This is the "advanced deep learning" option for the report.
  - Downsides: with 24 nodes and rare events, it often doesn't beat LightGBM. It
    also takes longer to train and tune.

  4. Temporal Fusion Transformer / LSTM

  - Multi-horizon deep forecasting with attention, so you can see which past
    time steps it relies on.
  - It can work, but it's heavy for binary rare-event data, and it doesn't use
    the graph structure explicitly.

  5. LightGBM plus Telegram text features (the optional part of the task)

  - Add counts of keywords like "Шахед", "Ту-95", "пуски" or "курсом на
    <oblast>" from posts published before t.
  - This is the biggest potential improvement, because monitoring channels often
    report launches before the official alert.

  Suggested combination

  1. LightGBM (spatial) as the main model, with an ablation that compares own
     history only against own history plus neighbours.
  2. Hawkes for interpretation and the propagation map.
  3. If time allows, LightGBM plus Telegram to cover the optional part.

  That's enough complexity for a strong project. Skip the GNN unless you have
  time left, because it's a lot of extra work for an uncertain gain.

  **Main findings:**
  - **Hawkes models (crime and terrorism):**
    - Mohler et al. (JASA 2011) modelled crime as a background rate plus a
  self-exciting part, the same structure as earthquake-aftershock (ETAS) models.
    - In Mohler et al.'s 2015 randomised field trials (JASA), the ETAS model
  predicted 1.4–2.2x as much crime as human analysts. Patrols guided by it cut
  crime by 7.4% per unit of patrol time.
    - Porter & White (Annals of Applied Statistics 2012), on daily attacks in
  Indonesia, split the problem into "is there any attack" and "how many". That
  is a close match to rare alert onsets.
    - I found no peer-reviewed head-to-head comparison of Hawkes models against
  gradient boosting.
  - **tick on Python 3.12:** unconfirmed. The README only says "Python 3.5 or
  newer", automated testing is switched off, and 78 issues are open. I couldn't
  load the PyPI page, so I couldn't check for 3.12 wheels. I didn't check
  hawkeslib's status. Test the install in a 3.12 environment; if it fails,
  writing your own exponential-kernel Hawkes in NumPy or PyTorch is a short job.
  - **Neural point-process models:** EasyTPP (ICLR 2024) is the standard library
  and benchmark. An independent study across 15 real datasets (Bosser & Ben
  Taieb, TMLR 2023) found that earlier papers compared models in inconsistent
  setups. It also found the neural models' event-type probabilities are poorly
  calibrated. Neither source shows them consistently beating classic Hawkes. I
  didn't get EasyTPP's results tables.
  - **Graph neural networks:** their advantage is mostly reported on dense
  traffic benchmarks. SimST (2023) matched them with a simpler model, and
  gradient boosting was competitive in the Traffic4cast 2022 challenge. I found
  no evidence that they beat gradient boosting on a small graph with rare binary
  targets. With 24 regions, LightGBM's neighbour-lag features already encode
  the graph, so a GNN fits best as one member of an ensemble.
  - **Conflict forecasting systems:**
    - ViEWS (Uppsala/PRIO) is built as an ensemble of models, with some models
  specialised for near horizons and others for distant ones.
    - In ViEWS's escalation competition, a simple ensemble beat the average
  individual model, and the 2023/24 challenge moved to probabilistic forecasts.
    - Mueller & Rauh (JEEA 2022) show that onsets in previously calm places are
  the hardest case.
    - ACLED CAST forecasts 4-week event counts by province. Its own reports show
  the biggest misses during sudden escalations (+24% in October 2023). I found
  no independent evaluation of CAST.
    - The lessons for you: evaluate on true future windows, score onsets after
  quiet periods separately from continuations, and report calibrated
  probabilities.
  - **Earthquake analogy:** machine-learning models roughly match or slightly
  beat ETAS and run far faster. ETAS is described as "brittle" to fit, which is
  a warning for fitting Hawkes on your alert data. A hybrid preprint (CL-ETAS)
  reports ETAS undercounting events in three Southern California aftershock
  sequences.
  - **Hawkes intensity as a LightGBM feature:** I found no published study doing
  exactly this. Related hybrids exist: Boost-R and an LSTM-Hawkes model that
  reported 27–84% lower error. Suggested recipe:
    - Fit the Hawkes model on the training window only.
    - At each prediction time, compute the intensity using only earlier events,
  then turn it into an onset probability for the 15/30/60-min horizon as 1 −
  exp(−∫λ).
    - Add that to LightGBM's features.
    - A cheaper version is past alerts per region counted with exponential decay
  at several rates; these are the quantities a Hawkes model is built from.
    - Either way, it needs validating on your data.

  Findings by question:

  1. **Class imbalance.** The LightGBM docs say `is_unbalance` and
  `scale_pos_weight` "should increase the overall performance metric" but give
  "poor estimates of the individual class probabilities". Undersampling has the
  same effect: it leaves the ranking intact but biases calibration (Dal Pozzolo
  et al. 2015, which gives an analytical correction). So if we re-weight or
  undersample, we should recalibrate on a later time block before publishing
  probabilities.

  2. **Metrics.**
     - Saito & Rehmsmeier 2015 show that ROC plots can be deceptive on
  imbalanced data and PR curves are more informative.
     - scikit-learn notes that Brier score and log loss mix calibration with
  resolution, so a lower Brier score does not by itself mean better calibration.
  Use the decomposition or reliability diagrams.
     - Platt (sigmoid) calibration is better for small samples. Isotonic is as
  good or better only above about 1000 samples (Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana 2005,
  as cited by scikit-learn). For oblasts with a 0.3% base rate, that points to a
  pooled or sigmoid calibrator rather than per-oblast isotonic.
     - Report both macro and pooled averages, with metrics normalized by base
  rate.

  3. **Cross-validation and significance.**
     - fpp3 describes the rolling forecasting origin, and Bergmeir & Benítez
  2012 recommend blocked CV. Both argue for several walk-forward folds rather
  than a single test month.
     - Diebold-Mariano (1995) with the Harvey-Leybourne-Newbold (1997)
  small-sample correction is implemented in R `forecast::dm.test` and
  statsmodels.
     - The standard DM test loses reliability when the loss difference varies
  over time (Harvey, Leybourne & Zu), which applies under drift.

  4. **Concept drift.** Gama et al. 2014 (ACM Computing Surveys) is the standard
  survey. A concrete drift risk: in August 2026 the Air Force cut back its
  ballistic-missile reporting, so features derived from those reports can change
  without warning.

  5. **Early-warning metrics.** Earthquake early-warning evaluations score
  alerts as matched, missed, false or late, and measure lead time.
     - ShakeAlert 2.0 had false/missed rates of 8%/16% for M5+ events.
     - Minson et al. argue that accepting more false alerts gives the best
  overall performance.
